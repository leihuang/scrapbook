# Tractatus Atomico-Habitus

The ideas of James Clear's *Atomic Habits*, set in the form of Wittgenstein's *Tractatus*.

---

## 1 — The self is the sum of its repetitions.

- **1.1** What a person does once is an event. What a person does daily is a person.
- **1.2** There are three depths at which change may be attempted: the outcome, the process, the identity.
  - **1.2.1** The first asks *what do I want*. The second, *what must I do*. The third, *who do I wish to be*.
  - **1.2.2** Change begun at the outcome is the most common and the least durable. Change begun at the identity outlasts the reason for it.
- **1.3** Every action is a vote cast for some version of oneself.
  - **1.3.1** No single vote decides the election. None is wasted.
  - **1.3.2** A majority suffices. One need not be unanimous to be someone.
- **1.4** A belief held without behavior is only a rumor about oneself.
  - **1.4.1** Identity is not declared but accumulated. The proof precedes the conviction.
- **1.5** The circle closes: what one believes oneself to be, one does; what one does, one becomes.
  - **1.5.1** Since the circle turns either way, the only question is which one to enter.

> **Explanation.** A habit matters less for what it produces than for what it proves. The runner is not made by the race but by the running; each repetition is evidence that a certain identity is true, and once believed, the identity makes further action cheap. So the starting question is not "what do I want to achieve" but "who would already be doing this, and what is the smallest act that proves I am them."

---

## 2 — Change is not an event but a rate.

- **2.1** A small margin, taken once, is indistinguishable from nothing. Taken enough times, it is indistinguishable from transformation.
  - **2.1.1** Improvement compounds. So does decay. Neither announces itself at the time.
  - **2.1.2** The direction of repeated action counts for more than the size of any single action.
- **2.2** Progress is accumulated before it is visible.
  - **2.2.1** The Plateau of Latent Potential is the interval in which the work has already succeeded but has not yet appeared.
  - **2.2.2** Most abandonment happens there. The delay is not a fault in the method; it is the shape of all accumulation.
- **2.3** Goals set the direction. Systems produce the motion.
  - **2.3.1** The goal is shared by everyone who wants the thing. Only the system distinguishes those who get it.
  - **2.3.2** A goal reached without a system is a loan; a goal reached by a system is a dividend.
  - **2.3.3** To arrive at a summit by luck is to remain a person who cannot climb.
- **2.4** Goals are temporary by construction: achieved, they stop guiding; unachieved, they make contentment conditional on a date.
  - **2.4.1** What one wants is never the outcome but the permanent capacity to produce it.

> **Explanation.** Nothing dramatic happens on any given day, which is why the arithmetic works and why people quit. Attention on outcomes produces effort in bursts and relief at the finish; attention on the process produces a machine that keeps running afterward. Ask less often *what do I want to achieve*, and more often *what process would produce this as a by-product*.

---

## 3 — A habit is a solved problem stored in the body.

- **3.1** The mind solves a problem once and thereafter delegates it.
  - **3.1.1** Automaticity is the reward for repetition and also its price: what runs without attention runs unexamined.
  - **3.1.2** Habits do not narrow freedom; they create it. Only what is automatic releases the mind for something else.
- **3.2** Every habit runs one circuit: cue, craving, response, reward.
  - **3.2.1** The cue predicts. The craving gives the prediction force. The response acts, but only if it is easy enough to be possible. The reward satisfies, and in satisfying, teaches the cue to be noticed again.
  - **3.2.2** Break any link and the circuit does not run — for the habits one wants and for those one does not.
- **3.3** What forms a habit is repetition, not duration.
  - **3.3.1** The unit of measurement is frequency. Never elapsed time.

> **Explanation.** A habit is the brain's refusal to solve the same problem twice. The loop is therefore both diagnosis and lever set: to build, strengthen each link; to break, sever the weakest. And since repetition is the mechanism, "how many times" is the only honest progress report.

---

## 4 — What is obvious, attractive, easy, and satisfying will be done.

What is invisible, unappealing, difficult, and unsatisfying will not. Four laws, one per link of the loop.

### 4.1 First law: make it obvious.

- **4.1.1** One cannot change a behavior one has not noticed. List what is already done, mark each as helpful, harmful, or neutral. Awareness precedes intention.
- **4.1.2** **Implementation intention.** *I will [behavior] at [time] in [place].* An intention without coordinates is a wish; the more exactly specified, the less deliberation required at the hour of action.
- **4.1.3** **Habit stacking.** An existing habit is a reliable cue: *After [current habit], I will [new habit].* Fasten the unfamiliar to the familiar.
- **4.1.4** Stacks may be chained, until a morning is a structure rather than a series of decisions. But the join must match in place and tempo; a cue that fires at the wrong moment is not a cue.
- **4.1.5** Make the object conspicuous. What is in view is in mind; what is put away is forgotten.

### 4.2 Second law: make it attractive.

- **4.2.1** We act on the anticipation of reward, not the reward. Desire is the engine; pleasure only the receipt.
- **4.2.2** **Temptation bundling.** *After [needed habit], I may [wanted activity].* The craving is borrowed until the habit generates its own.
- **4.2.3** Enter a group in which the desired behavior is unremarkable. The strongest craving is the craving to belong, and shared behavior is attractive whether or not it is good.
- **4.2.4** A behavior that earns approval is repeated; one that costs status is abandoned, however sound.
- **4.2.5** Reframe the difficulty as a privilege — not *I have to* but *I get to*. The same act admits many descriptions, and they do not cost the same.

### 4.3 Third law: make it easy.

- **4.3.1** Motion is not action. Planning, researching, preparing are the more comfortable failures.
- **4.3.2** Behavior follows the path of least friction. Shorten the path to the good; lengthen the path to the bad.
- **4.3.3** Prepare the environment in advance, so the desired action is ready before motivation is needed.
- **4.3.4** **The Two-Minute Rule.** Let the entry cost be two minutes. *Read every night* becomes *read one page*. The gateway must be small enough to begin without resistance; its purpose is not the result but the showing up.
- **4.3.5** A habit must exist before it can be improved. Standardize, then optimize.
- **4.3.6** **Commitment devices.** Bind the future self while the present self is still reasonable. A single decision may govern a thousand later ones; such decisions are habits purchased outright.

### 4.4 Fourth law: make it satisfying.

- **4.4.1** What is rewarded immediately is repeated. What is punished immediately is abandoned.
- **4.4.2** The costs of a good habit are present and its rewards deferred; for a bad habit the order reverses. This asymmetry is the whole of the difficulty.
- **4.4.3** **Habit tracking.** Manufacture an immediate ending: a mark, a count, a chain one can see. The mark serves three laws at once — it records the cue, proves the identity, and pays on the spot.
- **4.4.4** Do not break the chain. Consistency becomes its own reward once it is long enough to be lost.
- **4.4.5** Never miss twice. One lapse is an accident; the second founds a new habit.
- **4.4.6** The record is a servant. Never preserve the number at the expense of the thing it counts.
- **4.4.7** Attach an immediate cost to failure and a witness to the promise. What is watched is done.

### 4.5 The inversions.

- **4.5.1** Make the cue invisible: remove it, or move yourself.
- **4.5.2** Make the craving unattractive: reframe what the habit actually gives you.
- **4.5.3** Make the response difficult: add steps, remove tools, put the object out of reach.
- **4.5.4** Make the reward unsatisfying: let the cost arrive at once, in the presence of someone whose opinion matters.

> **Explanation.** The four laws are the loop turned into instructions. They are not techniques to choose among but four questions to ask of any behavior: can I see it, do I want it, is it easy, does it pay now? Each named method answers one — intentions and stacking supply the cue, bundling and belonging the craving, the two-minute rule and friction the response, tracking and immediate cost the reward. A habit that keeps failing is failing at one of the four, usually the third.

---

## 5 — The environment is the invisible hand.

- **5.1** One does not choose among all possible actions, only among visible ones.
  - **5.1.1** Every environment is a collection of cues, whether or not it was designed as one.
- **5.2** Self-control is a strategy of the short term only.
  - **5.2.1** The disciplined are not those who resist temptation more often, but those who encounter it less often.
  - **5.2.2** Do not ask willpower to fight architecture. It loses on the days that matter.
- **5.3** Assign each space a single meaning. A place that means two things will be used for the easier one.
  - **5.3.1** Where the association cannot be broken, move. A new context is the cheapest new self.
- **5.4** We imitate the close, the many, and the powerful.
  - **5.4.1** The habits of one's company become one's own by default, without decision and without notice.

> **Explanation.** Willpower is a poor instrument because it must be spent at the moment of greatest weakness; context is a better one because it is arranged in advance, in a calm hour. "Why can't I stick to this?" is nearly always better answered by looking at the room, the phone, and the friends than by looking inward.

---

## 6 — Talent sets the range. Habits decide the position within it.

- **6.1** Choose the game in which your nature is already an advantage. Effort spent against one's grain buys less.
  - **6.1.1** Inheritance explains differences between people. It does none of their work.
- **6.2** The chief obstacle to mastery is not failure but boredom.
  - **6.2.1** Novelty always fades. The professional keeps the schedule anyway; that is the entire distinction.
  - **6.2.2** **The Goldilocks Rule.** Attention is greatest when the task sits just past current ability. Raise the difficulty as competence rises, and keep some always in reserve.
- **6.3** Habit makes the behavior possible without attention; deliberate practice returns the attention on purpose.
  - **6.3.1** Automaticity unreviewed becomes decline. What once improved you may quietly stop improving you.
  - **6.3.2** Measure, then reflect. Habits produce the behavior; reflection keeps it correct.
- **6.4** A lapse does not erase accumulated evidence. The habit is not destroyed by one bad day.
  - **6.4.1** Speed of return matters more than perfection of record.
  - **6.4.2** After failure, examine the system rather than the character. Ask what made the wrong behavior likely, and change that.
- **6.5** Hold identity broadly. An identity built on a single role is destroyed when the role ends.

> **Explanation.** Habits carry you to competence and then quietly cap you there, because what made the behavior easy also made it invisible to correction. Mastery is the loop plus a review: the habit to do the work, the reflection to keep it honest, the rising difficulty to keep it interesting.

---

### The procedure, in order

*Decide who you wish to become. Find the smallest behavior that proves it. Attach it to a cue you already obey. Remove every step between intention and action. Give it an ending you can see. Repeat until the repetition is unremarkable. When you fail, return at once. When it becomes easy, make it harder.*

---

## 7 — What cannot be sustained by will must be arranged by design.

> **Explanation.** The whole book reduces to this. Motivation is real but unreliable, and an architecture founded on it collapses precisely on the days that decide the outcome. Everything above is the work of making the desired behavior the default, so that on the day you have nothing left to give, nothing is required.
